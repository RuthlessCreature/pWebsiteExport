China sourcing buyer guide

Verify the Chinese supplier before the order becomes expensive to unwind.

Supplier verification is not one certificate, one video call or one business-license screenshot. The objective is to connect the legal company, the people communicating with you, the production route, the product capability and the payment relationship into one coherent picture before you commit significant money or time.

Buyer control points

A process that can be checked, not guessed.

Use this guide as an operating framework and adapt the depth of control to product risk, order value, customization and destination-market requirements.

Working checklist

  • Legal company name
  • Business role: factory/trader/hybrid
  • Factory and production address
  • Critical in-house processes
  • Subcontracted processes
  • Relevant product evidence
  • Quality inspection method
  • Certificate scope where applicable
  • Quotation and contract entity
  • Bank beneficiary consistency
  • References or export evidence where useful
  • Known gaps and open questions
01

Confirm the legal and commercial identity

Record the company name used on quotations, contracts, invoices and payment instructions. Check whether those names are consistent and clarify any difference between a sales entity, factory entity and export company. The goal is not to reject every multi-entity structure; it is to understand who is responsible for what and where money is going.

02

Understand whether you are dealing with a factory, trader or hybrid

Ask which processes are performed in-house and which are subcontracted. Many legitimate suppliers use subcontractors for coating, heat treatment, printing, packaging or specialized processes. Hidden subcontracting becomes risky when the buyer assumes a process is controlled by the quoted company but the company cannot demonstrate how the external process is selected and checked.

03

Verify product and process fit

Look for evidence tied to your exact manufacturing problem: similar material, tolerance, electrical architecture, tooling, volume, surface process or assembly complexity. A factory that makes an adjacent product may still be a good fit, but generic catalogue breadth is weaker evidence than process-specific capability.

04

Check quality-control capability

Ask how incoming material, in-process work and final goods are checked. For technical products, request examples of inspection records or test methods. The sophistication required should match the product risk: a simple household item does not need the same system as a precision assembly, but both need an objective way to identify nonconforming goods.

05

Review certificates and claims in context

Do not treat a certificate logo as proof that every product and configuration is covered. Confirm the entity name, scope, product family, model or process where relevant, and whether the document is current. Where compliance matters to your market, the buyer should independently confirm the exact evidence required for the intended product and destination.

06

Assess communication quality as operational evidence

Fast replies are not the same as good communication. Strong suppliers can answer structured questions, identify ambiguity, preserve revisions and explain exceptions. Repeatedly avoiding technical questions, changing answers without explanation or providing inconsistent company details are signals that deserve clarification before commitment.

07

Check payment identity and commercial terms

Treat unexpected changes in bank details, beneficiary name or payment destination as a stop-and-verify event. Align the commercial entity, contract and payment instruction before transfer. Deposits, tooling payments and balance release should follow an agreed milestone rather than urgency created by chat messages.
